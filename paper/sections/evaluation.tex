\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \tool{} on real serverless workflows, asking: can it detect the six
defect classes before deployment (RQ1); can it recover the semantics \asl{} omits
cheaply enough to analyse existing workflows (RQ2); and what does verification cost,
at build and run time (RQ3)? Every number below is produced by the released tool over
a committed corpus and is reproducible with the commands shown.

\subsection{Setup and corpus (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval:corpus}

We mined \textbf{193} Step Functions workflows from two public AWS repositories
(\texttt{aws-samples/}\allowbreak\texttt{aws-stepfunctions-examples} and
\texttt{step-functions-}\allowbreak\texttt{workflows-collection}), taking every file
that parses as an \asl{} state machine. The corpus (Fig.~\ref{fig:eval}) is realistic and diverse:
1{,}355 states, median 6 per workflow (max 33), and broad use of \texttt{Choice} (166),
\texttt{Map} (64), \texttt{Parallel} (33), retries (84 workflows), and catch handlers
(51). All experiments ran on a commodity laptop (Rust~1.96 release build).

\subsection{Findings in the wild (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval:wild}

Run unmodified over the corpus (with inference enabled), \tool{} flags
\textbf{95 of 193} workflows. We separate \emph{sound native} findings, which need no
annotations, from \emph{inference-driven} findings, which are warnings requiring triage.
Among the native findings, nine workflows contain a \texttt{Choice} with no
\texttt{Default} (\code{SC0007})---a recognised cause of \texttt{States.NoChoiceMatched}
run-time failures---and one is genuinely malformed (\code{SC0010}): an aws-samples
example nests a top-level \texttt{QueryLanguage} directive \emph{inside} its
\texttt{States} block, where Step Functions would reject it. The temporal analysis adds
\textbf{33} findings, all \code{SC6001}: unbounded \texttt{waitForTaskToken}
callbacks---human-approval and payment/shipping steps with no timeout or heartbeat, a
real anti-pattern AWS documents against. The data-flow (\code{SC1101}, \code{SC1110}) and concurrency
(\code{SC5xxx}) analyses fire on \emph{none} of the 193: \code{SC1101} because the
analysis is sound and these samples are correct (Section~\ref{sec:eval:dataflow}),
\code{SC1110} because no corpus workflow guards a \texttt{Choice} on a field it never
produces, and \code{SC5xxx} because its conservative overwriting-write rule finds no
genuine last-writer-wins race among the corpus's (correctly keyed or commuting)
concurrent writes---all precision results, with detection power shown by the mutation
study below. The effect-aware compensation check \emph{does} surface a new class in the
wild: \code{SC4010} flags \textbf{28} persistent tasks whose only \texttt{Catch} merely
logs, notifies, or fails---never reaching an undo action---a finding a bare
``has-a-\texttt{Catch}'' rule misses entirely. The inference-driven warnings
(\code{SC3001}: 36; \code{SC4001}: 117; \code{SC4010}: 28) mark non-idempotent
tasks retried broadly and persistent tasks whose failure paths do not (or cannot)
compensate; as Section~\ref{sec:eval:infer} shows, the \code{SC3001}/\code{SC4001} class
has measured precision of 60\% on gold-covered findings (70\% for the compensation
warnings \code{SC4001}, 100\% for \code{SC4010}) but includes false positives (e.g.\ a
\texttt{RefundPayment} compensator inferred as needing its own compensation), which is
precisely why they are surfaced as warnings rather than errors.

\subsection{Mutation-based detection (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval:mut}

Because correct real workflows expose few of the deeper defects, we measure detection
power by \emph{error injection}. For each defect class we inject exactly one defect
per applicable workflow with \texttt{stepcheck mutate} and re-verify; a defect counts
as \emph{detected} only if the expected diagnostic's count strictly increases over the
unmutated baseline (so we measure detection of the \emph{injected} defect, not
pre-existing findings). Across \textbf{616} mutants spanning six classes---the four
above plus a shared-resource \texttt{Parallel} write (\code{SC5001}) and a
heartbeat\,$\geq$\,timeout (\code{SC6003})---\tool{} achieves \textbf{100\% recall}
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline}, \tool{} column), and it never reports the expected defect on
the corresponding originals. (The data-flow class is studied separately in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:dataflow} because, unlike the others, its detection depends on a
known document shape rather than firing on raw \asl{}.) We stress that this measures
\emph{reliability of each check on the defect class it targets}, not detection of
arbitrary bugs; the mutations are constructed to fall within each check's remit. To show
the checks are \emph{specific} and not merely trigger-happy, the harness also emits a
per-operator confusion matrix: it is near-diagonal---each semantic check fires only on its
own class (the retry mutant trips \code{SC3001} and nothing else; the concurrency mutant
only \code{SC5001})---and the only off-diagonal entries are genuine structural
\emph{consequences} of an edit (redirecting a transition also orphans its old target,
\code{SC0003}), not misfires of unrelated checks. To complement this synthetic study with
\emph{independently}-introduced defects, we also mine real fix-commit pairs and replay
\tool{} on them (\texttt{stepcheck eval-pairs}); this finds six genuine in-the-wild
defects, reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval:realbugs}. The next subsection first contrasts
the mutation results with what a standard validator detects on the same mutants.

\subsection{Comparison to a standard validator (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval:baseline}

The natural baseline is the validation a Step Functions developer already has.
\texttt{statelint}~\cite{awsStatelint} (the AWS Labs reference linter; we ran v0.8.0)
checks an \asl{} definition against the J2119/\asl{} schema plus structural rules.
We ran it on the same corpus and the same 616 mutants, scoring a mutant
\emph{detected} only when statelint emits a new problem that names the injected fault
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). The two tools \emph{agree} on the defects statelint can
express: it flags every dangling transition (166/166) and---because a broken JSONPath
binding violates the schema---almost every broken \emph{binding} (140/141). But it
detects \textbf{0} unsafe retries, \textbf{0} missing compensations, \textbf{0}
concurrency-interference defects, and \textbf{0} temporal-budget defects: a broad
\texttt{States.ALL} retry, an absent \texttt{Catch}, two branches writing one table, and
a heartbeat that exceeds a timeout are all schema-valid, so a validator without a notion
of idempotency, persistence, resources, or a temporal budget cannot see them. (A naive
count-of-problems metric would appear to ``detect'' many of these---the injected retry's
float \texttt{IntervalSeconds} once tripped statelint's integer pedantry, a removed
\texttt{Catch} orphans its handler state---but none of those complaints is the analysis
in question, so we do not credit them.) In the wild the asymmetry is starker still:
statelint flags 113/193 workflows with 499 problems, \textbf{410 of them} schema-shape
nits (e.g.\ \texttt{BackoffRate}/\texttt{IntervalSeconds} number typing) and none
touching the six semantic classes, whereas \tool{}'s findings are all semantically
meaningful. \tool{} is thus complementary to and strictly more discriminating than
schema validation on the properties that motivate it.

\subsection{Data-flow provenance: soundness, power, and reach (RQ1/RQ2)}
\label{sec:eval:dataflow}

The data-flow analysis (Section~\ref{sec:approach:dataflow}) is the paper's central new
check, and its claim is \emph{soundness}---no false positives---so we evaluate it on its
own terms. \emph{Precision.} Run over all 193 unmodified workflows, \code{SC1101} fires
\textbf{zero} times. These are mostly correct sample workflows, and the analysis is
conservative by construction; firing on none of them is the expected, desired outcome and
empirically corroborates the soundness statement of Section~\ref{sec:approach:dataflow}.
\emph{Independent oracle.} To validate soundness beyond the absence of findings, we
cross-check with a per-path \emph{execution oracle} (\texttt{stepcheck oracle}): a second
interpreter that shares no code with the analysis, enumerates the concrete document along
every acyclic path to a state, and confirms a flagged field is genuinely absent on all of
them. Over the \textbf{84} injected missing-field defects the typed tier detects (below),
the oracle confirms \emph{every one} absent on all reaching paths with \textbf{zero}
counterexamples---agreement between two independent algorithms, which is strong evidence
the transfer functions and join are sound.
\emph{Power.} That the check is not vacuous we show on worked demonstrators (released in
the corpus). On a \emph{native} example---no annotations---where a \texttt{Pass} builds
\texttt{\{order:\{orderId,amount\}\}} and a later task reads \texttt{\$.order.total},
\tool{} reports \code{SC1101} on raw \asl{}; \texttt{statelint} and \texttt{asl-validator}
accept it (the path is valid). On a \emph{typed} example with a three-field input schema,
it reports two \code{SC1101}: a schema miss (\texttt{\$.total} for \texttt{amount}) and a
\emph{flow-sensitive} miss---\texttt{\$.reservation.reservationCode} where an upstream
\texttt{ResultSelector}/\texttt{ResultPath} merge produced \texttt{reservationId}---a
defect an adjacent-task field-subset check cannot see because the field is created two
states earlier. \emph{Reach.} To measure detection at corpus scale we run the typed tier:
each workflow's start document is seeded as a closed record of the top-level fields it
references, and we inject a missing-field defect per workflow (redirecting a reference to
an absent field). \tool{} detects \textbf{84 of 126 (67\%)}; the undetected third are
references that sit \emph{downstream of an opaque task result}, where the document shape
has soundly collapsed to $\top$ and no missing-field claim is possible. This number is
therefore not a reliability gap but a measurement of ASL's intrinsic opacity: roughly
two-thirds of field references on real workflows are statically recoverable, and the
remainder are exactly what richer typed outputs (a \texttt{ResultSelector}, or CNCF's
declared output schemas, Section~\ref{sec:eval:cncf}) push back. The baseline produces no
spurious \code{SC1101} under the closed-world seed either, so the typed tier inherits the
native tier's precision.

\subsection{Real defects in the wild (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval:realbugs}

The mutation study measures reliability on \emph{injected} defects; to complement it with
\emph{independently}-introduced ones, we mined fix-commit pairs from public Step Functions
repositories (\texttt{eval/mine\_realbugs.js}: commits whose message looks like a fix and
whose diff edits an \asl{} definition; we extract the file \emph{before} and \emph{after}
the commit) and replayed \tool{} on each with \texttt{stepcheck eval-pairs}. On \textbf{7 of
39} mined pairs \tool{} reports a defect on the pre-fix version that the fix removes; manual
adjudication confirms \textbf{6} as genuine defects (the seventh is a \emph{count artifact}
---the fix deleted the flagged task rather than adding compensation---which we do not
credit). The six span five repositories (one an official AWS sample):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{\code{SC1101}} (\texttt{aws-batch-runtime-monitoring}): a task reads
        \texttt{\$.Execution.Input}---a single-\texttt{\$} path selecting a \emph{document}
        field no state produces, where the author meant the context object
        \texttt{\$\$.Execution.Input}; the fix routes the value through a real field.
  \item \textbf{\code{SC1101}} (\texttt{serverless-ai-fitness}): two tasks read
        \texttt{\$.profile.Item}/\texttt{\$.userId}, fields not produced at those states;
        the fix refactors the user-data flow onto produced fields.
  \item \textbf{\code{SC1110}} (\texttt{serverless-ai-fitness}): a \texttt{Choice} guards on
        \texttt{\$.profile.subscription.level}, a field never produced---so the subscriber
        branch is unreachable and subscribers were never routed; the fix corrects the path.
        Our path-sensitive dead-guard check thus finds a real \emph{logic} bug.
  \item \textbf{\code{SC6001}} (\texttt{leave-management}): a callback task has no
        \texttt{TimeoutSeconds}/\texttt{HeartbeatSeconds} and can hang indefinitely; the fix
        adds \texttt{TimeoutSeconds}.
  \item \textbf{\code{SC0002}} (\texttt{airway-shipment-orchestrator}): a transition targets
        an undefined state; the fix adds it.
  \item \textbf{\code{SC0003}/\code{SC0004}/\code{SC0005}} (\texttt{video-translation}): a
        state declares \texttt{"End": "True"}---the string, not the boolean---so it is not
        recognised as terminal, leaving a dead-end and unreachable states; the fix sets
        \texttt{"End": true}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Four of the six---the \code{SC1101}, \code{SC1110}, and \code{SC6001} catches---are
in classes no schema validator can express}; the other two are structural (a dangling
transition and a string-typed \texttt{End}) that \texttt{statelint} would also reject. On
the remaining 32 pairs---predominantly value/config corrections (a bucket name, an API
endpoint, a parameter) outside \tool's remit---it reports no fix-removed defect, and the
flagged code is \emph{absent} from every post-fix version (no residual false positive). This
is a genuine in-the-wild result on the \emph{deployed} artifact: the centrepiece provenance
analysis, the new dead-guard check, and the temporal and structural checks each catch a
real, independently-fixed defect. We release the miner and the adjudicated benchmark
(\texttt{corpus/realbugs/}) so it can be grown.

\subsection{Inference accuracy (RQ2)}
\label{sec:eval:infer}

The semantic checks depend on recovering idempotency and persistence, so we measure the
heuristic against a \emph{human} gold standard of \textbf{147 tasks across 23 workflows}
---spanning Sagas, an inventory domain, account vending, security automation, media/AI
pipelines, CQRS, data migration, and more--- labelled by each task's true operational
semantics (the annotators confidently label 137; the rest are abstentions). To guard
against labeller bias, the 13 workflows added in this revision were labelled by
\emph{two independent human annotators} under a written rubric and reconciled; agreement
was perfect (Cohen's $\kappa=1.00$ for \emph{both} idempotency and persistence over the
116 doubly-labelled tasks), and the original 10 were hand-labelled by an author. We
release every label, the rubric, and the scoring harness for scrutiny. The heuristic
abstains on
$\sim$20\% of tasks (unconventional verbs such as \emph{confirm}, \emph{audit},
\emph{aggregate}); on the rest it reaches \textbf{87.3\%} accuracy for persistence but
only \textbf{70.0\%} for idempotency (Table~\ref{tab:infer}). The asymmetry is the
headline result of this experiment, and it is honest: persistence (which drives the
compensation check) tracks resource-acquiring verbs well, whereas idempotency is
genuinely hard from names alone---\emph{cancel}/\emph{release} compensators and
stable-key writes (a DynamoDB \texttt{PutItem}/\texttt{UpdateItem} keyed by a primary
key) are idempotent \emph{in effect} but read as writes. That perfect inter-annotator
agreement ($\kappa=1.00$) coexists with imperfect heuristic accuracy means the truth is
clear to a human but the names do not carry it. This is precisely why inferred findings are \emph{warnings}: a one-line
annotation overrides any default, and the residual error is visible, not silent.

\paragraph{Precision of in-the-wild warnings.}
The gold set lets us measure the precision of the inference-driven warnings of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:wild} directly: for each warning that lands on a gold-labelled
task, a finding is a true positive iff the gold label confirms it. Over the 30 gold-%
covered \code{SC3001}/\code{SC4001} findings, \textbf{18 (60\%)} are true
positives---\textbf{16/23 (70\%)} for the compensation warnings \code{SC4001}, but only
\textbf{2/7 (29\%)} for the broad-retry warnings \code{SC3001}, mirroring the weaker
idempotency inference that drives them; the new effect-aware \code{SC4010} is
\textbf{6/6 (100\%)} on its gold-covered findings. Every false positive is a compensator
(\texttt{RefundPayment}, \texttt{CancelFlightReservation}) or an idempotent
keyed/metadata write---the same patterns above---which is exactly why these are surfaced
for triage rather than as errors. RQ2 answer: persistence is recovered well and the
compensation warnings are mostly actionable; idempotency-driven retry warnings are
noisier and benefit most from a declared annotation.

\subsection{Verification cost and runtime overhead (RQ3)}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

Verification is cheap: the eight passes---including the data-flow fixpoint---average
$\approx$\textbf{39}\,$\mu$s/workflow (median $\approx$25\,$\mu$s, max $\approx$1.1\,ms);
the whole corpus verifies in $\approx$\textbf{7.5\,ms}. The three new analyses roughly
doubled the per-workflow cost over the five-pass version yet kept it firmly
sub-millisecond on all but one workflow. Because all
analysis is ahead-of-deployment and the output is ordinary \asl{}, the \emph{runtime}
overhead is \textbf{zero}---no middleware, sidecar, or instrumentation---unlike runtime
fault-tolerance and transaction approaches~\cite{zhang2020beldi,burckhardt2021durable}.

\subsection{Conciseness and AWS round-trip}
\label{sec:eval:roundtrip}

The typed DSL is also concise: 20 declarative statements expand to a 119-line \asl{}
definition while additionally encoding five schemas, a four-edge protocol, and per-task
idempotency/persistence/compensation that \asl{} cannot express. To confirm
compatibility end-to-end, we deployed the DSL-verified order workflow to real AWS Step
Functions \emph{unmodified} (only binding \texttt{FunctionName} to a Lambda ARN) as an
Express machine: it reached \texttt{SUCCEEDED}, the input propagating through all four
steps, billed 500\,ms\,/\,64\,MB; the resources were then torn down. A verified \tool{}
workflow runs on the unmodified industrial platform.

\subsection{Cross-format generalization: CNCF Serverless Workflow}
\label{sec:eval:cncf}

To test generalization beyond AWS, we added a third frontend for the vendor-neutral
CNCF Serverless Workflow DSL~\cite{cncfServerlessWorkflow} and ran it on \textbf{66}
example workflows from the specification's repository. The frontend lowers the DSL's
tasks (\texttt{call}/\texttt{do}/\texttt{fork}/\texttt{switch}/\texttt{try}/\dots) to the
\emph{same} IR \emph{without changing the IR or any analysis pass}, and parses all 66
with no failures. The structural, typestate, retry-safety, compensation, concurrency,
and temporal analyses apply unchanged; only the \asl-specific JSONPath references the
data-flow pass mines stay silent---correctly, since CNCF uses runtime expressions, not
JSONPath, so \code{SC1101} and the binding checks (\code{SC1001}--\code{SC1003}) do not
fire. A mutation study detects every injected defect with an injection site (retry 4/4,
structural 9/9, concurrency 1/1, temporal 50/50). Crucially, CNCF can declare JSON
Schemas, so where it does the typed contract check (\code{SC1010}) runs at \emph{full
precision with no inference}: a reordered schema-annotated order workflow is rejected with
three \code{SC1010} errors from the declared schemas---the same defect inference only
approximates on raw \asl, here exact. The asymmetry is informative: CNCF makes data
contracts explicit but, like \asl, leaves idempotency and compensation implicit, so those
checks still rely on annotation or inference in both ecosystems.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\emph{Construct.} The mutation study reports 100\% recall \emph{by design}: each
mutation targets a defect within a check's remit, so the result attests to the
reliability of each check on its own defect class, not to coverage of all possible
bugs---and we headline it only with that scope. We report it alongside the in-the-wild
findings, which are not constructed. \emph{Internal (gold labels).} The idempotency/persistence gold labels are human
judgements; the 13 workflows added in this revision were labelled by two independent
annotators under a written rubric with perfect agreement ($\kappa=1.00$ for both
properties), the original 10 were hand-labelled by an author, and we release all labels,
the rubric, and the scoring harness. The contested cases (cancel, refund, keyed writes)
are exactly where the heuristic errs against the human labels, and we report the
resulting warning precision rather than hide it. \emph{External.} The corpus is drawn
from AWS sample repositories, which may over-represent didactic workflows; we mitigate
this by taking \emph{all} parseable workflows rather than a curated subset, yielding a
realistic spread of sizes and features. \emph{Soundness.} The data-flow and structural
checks are conservative (they flag only definite faults), corroborated by zero
\code{SC1101} false positives on the corpus; typestate and typed-contract precision
depend on annotation quality, which is why \tool{} reports inferred findings as
warnings. The concurrency and temporal-budget analyses are \emph{warnings}: the
concurrency check is deliberately conservative (only last-writer-wins overwrites, never
commuting or item-keyed writes), which is why it raises zero in-the-wild false positives
while still detecting every injected race; a missing callback timeout (\code{SC6001})
may occasionally be acceptable for a short-lived task, so it too prompts review rather
than asserting a bug. The budget check \code{SC6002} did not fire in the wild (no corpus
workflow declares a machine timeout tight enough to be overrun); we keep it as a
defensive check and leave a labelled-precision study of the warning classes to future
work.
